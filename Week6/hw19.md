My team worked on **Citi's Payments & Digital Wallet Platform**, a backend banking platform that supports mobile money movement features like internal transfers, ACH payments, P2P payments, bill pay, wallet ledger updates, fraud checks, and real-time payment notifications for retail banking customers.

## Part 2 - A Day in the Life

A typical day started with our 9:30 AM Daily Standup. Michael, our Scrum Master, ran the meeting and kept it to around 15 minutes. Each person gave a quick update: what they finished yesterday, what they planned to do today, and whether they had any blockers.

When it was my turn, I might say, "Yesterday I finished the Spring Boot validation logic for payment limits and duplicate transaction detection. Today I'm working on publishing the AUTHORIZED payment event to Kafka and adding retry handling. My blocker is that I need confirmation from Priya and the risk team about whether a failed fraud score should stop the payment immediately or move it into manual review."

After standup, Priya would follow up with the risk stakeholder and clarify the business rule. That was important because in banking, we could not guess payment behavior. A small misunderstanding could affect compliance, customer money movement, or fraud exposure.

Then I usually checked Jira for my ticket details and GitHub for pull request comments. If Arjun reviewed my code, he might ask me to make the payment orchestration flow more idempotent so the same request could not accidentally create duplicate postings. If Ananya from QA found an edge case, like two payments happening at the same time from the same wallet balance, I would work with Pravin to make sure Redis counters and optimistic locking handled the race condition properly.

Around mid-day, we might have a short technical discussion. For example, if we were adding wallet hold and release logic, Pravin would explain the ledger impact, Neha would check whether any sensitive data needed masking, and Liam would confirm whether the deployment required a new secret or environment variable in AWS Secrets Manager.

In the afternoon, I would finish implementation, run JUnit and Mockito tests, verify Kafka event behavior locally or in a lower environment, and open a pull request. I would tag Arjun for backend review and Ananya for QA visibility. If the change touched mobile behavior, Ethan would confirm that the API response matched what the mobile app expected.

Sometimes issues came up late in the day. For example, if the Jenkins pipeline failed during deployment to staging, Liam would lead the investigation. If it was a Docker, ECS, or environment issue, he owned it. If the failure came from my service health check or bad configuration, I would help fix it immediately. Michael would keep the blocker visible so the team knew whether sprint work was at risk.

That was the daily rhythm: Jira for tracking, GitHub for code review, Slack and Zoom for communication, Confluence for technical notes, Jenkins and GitHub Actions for CI/CD, AWS CloudWatch for logs and metrics, and Kafka dashboards for event monitoring. The key was that we stayed aligned through frequent communication and did not let blockers stay hidden.

## Part 3 - Interview Q&A

### 1. What were you building and who was it for?

My team worked on Citi's Payments & Digital Wallet Platform. It supported money movement features for retail banking customers, including internal transfers, ACH payments, P2P payments, bill pay, wallet ledger updates, fraud checks, and payment notifications.

### 2. How many people were on your team and why?

My immediate Agile team had about nine people. That size worked well because we had product, Scrum, backend, QA, DevOps, design, and mobile integration coverage, but we were still small enough to communicate quickly.

### 3. Who was on your team?

Our Product Owner was Priya Raman, our Scrum Master was Michael Thompson, and I worked as a Java backend developer. Arjun Patel was a senior backend engineer, Neha Sharma handled security and compliance-related backend work, Pravin Nair worked on wallet ledger and reconciliation, Ethan Miller helped with frontend and mobile API integration, Ananya Gupta was QA, Sofia Martinez supported UX flows, and Liam O'Connor handled DevOps and deployments.

### 4. Who was your Product Owner and what did they do?

Our Product Owner was Priya Raman. She owned the product backlog, clarified requirements, worked with business stakeholders, prioritized payment features, and made sure acceptance criteria were clear before engineering started.

### 5. Who was your Scrum Master and how were they different from a project manager?

Our Scrum Master was Michael Thompson. He was different from a project manager because he did not assign tasks or control the team. He focused on Agile process, removing blockers, protecting sprint scope, and helping the team improve.

### 6. How many engineers did you have and how were they split?

We had several engineers, mostly backend because this was a backend-heavy banking platform. I was a Java backend developer. Arjun, Neha, and Pravin also worked on backend services. Ethan focused more on mobile and frontend API integration.

### 7. Did you have QA? Was testing only QA's job?

Yes, Ananya was our QA engineer, but quality was not only her job. Developers wrote unit, integration, and contract tests. QA helped with regression, edge cases, and release confidence, but the whole team owned quality.

### 8. Who handled deployments and infrastructure?

Liam, our DevOps engineer, owned deployment pipelines, AWS ECS/EKS, Jenkins, GitHub Actions, CloudWatch monitoring, and rollback support. But if a deployment failed because of my service, I helped fix it.

### 9. Did you have a designer?

Yes, Sofia worked with product and mobile teams on payment flow design. She usually worked ahead of the sprint so engineering had user flows and screen behavior before implementation.

### 10. How long were your sprints?

We used two-week sprints. That gave us enough time to deliver meaningful backend work, but still allowed regular feedback and adjustment.

### 11. What happened during Sprint Planning?

Priya presented the highest-priority backlog items. We reviewed requirements, asked questions, estimated effort, discussed dependencies, and committed only to work we believed we could finish in the sprint.

### 12. What did Daily Standup look like?

Daily Standup was about 15 minutes. Michael ran it. Each person shared yesterday's work, today's plan, and blockers. If something needed deeper discussion, we took it offline after standup.

### 13. What was Sprint Review?

Sprint Review was where we showed completed work to product and stakeholders. For backend features, we might demo API behavior, payment status changes, Kafka events, or a flow through the mobile app.

### 14. What was Retrospective?

Retrospective was where we talked honestly about the sprint process. We discussed what went well, what caused delays, and what we wanted to improve. We always tried to leave with concrete action items.

### 15. What was Backlog Refinement?

Backlog Refinement was where we reviewed upcoming work before Sprint Planning. Priya explained business value, engineers asked technical questions, QA raised test scenarios, and we made sure tickets were ready.

### 16. What was your Definition of Done?

Done meant code was implemented, reviewed, tested, merged, deployed to a lower environment, and accepted against the ticket criteria. For payment work, it also meant logs, monitoring, security, and failure handling were considered.

### 17. What would you do if you found a critical bug two days before release?

I would immediately tell Michael, Priya, QA, and the engineer who owned that area. We would create a critical bug ticket, reproduce it, assess customer and compliance impact, and decide whether to fix immediately or delay release.

### 18. What if a stakeholder wanted a feature mid-sprint?

We would not automatically add it. Priya would evaluate the urgency. If it was truly critical, we might swap it with another sprint item. Otherwise, it would go into the product backlog for a future sprint.

### 19. What if you were blocked and could not finish a ticket?

I would make the blocker visible right away in standup, Slack, and Jira. If it was business ambiguity, I would go to Priya. If it was technical, I would go to the right engineer, like Arjun, Pravin, Neha, or Liam.

### 20. What if two engineers disagreed on a technical approach?

We would compare the tradeoffs: reliability, performance, maintainability, compliance risk, and delivery timeline. If needed, we would involve the senior engineer or write a short technical design note before deciding.

### 21. What if the designer was out sick and you needed assets?

I would check Figma and existing design patterns first. If the flow was already clear, I would continue. If not, I would ask Priya whether to use an existing pattern temporarily or move to another ticket.

### 22. What if the deployment pipeline broke?

Liam would lead the investigation because DevOps owned the pipeline. But the whole team would help if needed. If my service caused a health check failure or bad configuration, I would work with Liam to fix it.

### 23. What was the Product Backlog?

The Product Backlog was the full list of features, bugs, compliance work, technical improvements, and production issues. Priya owned it, but engineers, QA, and stakeholders could suggest items.

### 24. What was the Sprint Backlog?

The Sprint Backlog was the set of tickets the team committed to for the current sprint. It was created during Sprint Planning from the highest-priority ready backlog items.

### 25. When did you write technical design docs?

We wrote technical design docs for larger changes, like new payment flows, ledger updates, Kafka event changes, or integrations with fraud and core banking systems. Backend leads reviewed the design, and product or compliance reviewed business impact when needed.

### 26. How did your team file and track bugs?

We tracked bugs in Jira. A good bug included steps to reproduce, expected result, actual result, environment, logs, screenshots if useful, severity, and owner.

### 27. Where did your team write things down?

We used Jira for tickets, Confluence for documentation, GitHub for pull requests, Slack for quick communication, Zoom for meetings, Figma for design, and CloudWatch or dashboards for operational visibility.

### 28. How did your team decide what went into the next sprint?

Priya prioritized based on business value, customer impact, compliance needs, and dependencies. The engineering team then checked capacity, complexity, and technical risk before committing.

### 29. What happened if the team consistently did not finish sprint work?

We discussed it in Retrospective. Usually it meant we were overcommitting, tickets were too large, requirements were unclear, or dependencies were not handled early enough. Then we adjusted planning and refinement.

### 30. How did a new feature go from idea to production?

A feature started as a business or customer need. Priya added it to the backlog, we refined it, designed the technical approach, implemented it in Spring Boot, wrote tests, reviewed the code, deployed to staging, verified with QA, and then released through the CI/CD pipeline.

### 31. How would you describe your team in 60 seconds?

I worked on a cross-functional Agile team for Citi's Payments & Digital Wallet Platform. My role was Java backend developer, mainly building Spring Boot microservices for payment initiation, validation, fraud checks, wallet ledger updates, Kafka events, and integrations with core banking systems. Our team had a Product Owner, Scrum Master, backend engineers, QA, DevOps, design support, and mobile integration support. We worked in two-week sprints using Jira, GitHub, Jenkins, AWS, Slack, and Confluence. If I had business ambiguity, I went to our Product Owner, Priya. If I had a technical blocker, I worked with engineers like Arjun, Neha, Pravin, or Liam depending on the area. The main focus of the team was delivering secure, reliable, compliant payment features while staying aligned through Agile ceremonies and strong communication.
